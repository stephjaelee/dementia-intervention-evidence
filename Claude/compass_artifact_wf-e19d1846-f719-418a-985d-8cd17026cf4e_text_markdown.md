# Lifestyle interventions match or exceed most dementia drug effects

**For a 75-year-old woman with MMSE 13-17 and mixed Alzheimer's/vascular pathology, the evidence strongly supports that lifestyle interventions—exercise, cognitive stimulation, and vascular risk management—produce effect sizes comparable to or exceeding most pharmacological treatments.** The new anti-amyloid therapies (lecanemab, donanemab) are not approved for moderate dementia and cannot be used in this patient. Meanwhile, cholinesterase inhibitors show modest benefits (Cohen's d ~0.3-0.4), and exercise interventions demonstrate similar or larger effects (Hedges' g 0.46), making the case that "lifestyle is medicine" is not hyperbole but evidence-based reality.

The critical finding for this patient: **no drugs are FDA-approved specifically for vascular dementia**, and effect sizes for all medications are 30-50% smaller in vascular/mixed pathology than in pure Alzheimer's. This makes optimizing blood pressure, continuing statins, and implementing structured exercise and cognitive stimulation programs not merely "adjunctive" but **primary therapeutic strategies**.

## Cholinesterase inhibitors provide modest but real benefits

Donepezil, rivastigmine, and galantamine all demonstrate statistically significant but modest cognitive benefits in moderate Alzheimer's disease. The pivotal trials—Rogers et al. 1998 for donepezil, Corey-Bloom et al. 1998 for rivastigmine, and Raskind et al. 2000 for galantamine—consistently show **ADAS-cog improvements of 2.5-3.1 points** versus placebo over 24-26 weeks.

The pooled weighted mean differences from Hansen et al.'s 2008 meta-analysis are remarkably consistent: donepezil -2.67 points (95% CI: -3.28 to -2.06), rivastigmine -3.01 points (95% CI: -3.80 to -2.21), and galantamine -2.76 points (95% CI: -3.17 to -2.34). These translate to approximately **6-9 months of preserved cognitive function** in a 6-month trial—meaningful but not dramatic.

Response rates hover around **50-60%** for any improvement, with NNT of 7-12 for global improvement and 3-7 for stabilization (no worsening). However, benefits are symptomatic rather than disease-modifying: after 6-12 months, decline resumes along a trajectory that remains above untreated patients but continues downward. The Swedish Alzheimer Treatment Study showed treated patients decline approximately **1.27 MMSE points per year** versus 1.07-3.4 points untreated.

Tolerability differs meaningfully between agents. Donepezil shows the lowest withdrawal rate due to adverse events (**11%**), versus galantamine (14%) and rivastigmine (**21%**). GI side effects—nausea, vomiting, diarrhea—are most pronounced with rivastigmine (nausea 44%, vomiting 30%) and least with donepezil (nausea 11%, vomiting 7%). For this reason, **donepezil is generally preferred as first-line therapy**, though rivastigmine may offer slight advantages for functional outcomes in more severe disease.

## Memantine adds value in moderate-to-severe disease

Memantine (Namenda) is specifically indicated for moderate-to-severe Alzheimer's disease and shows consistent, though modest, benefits in this population. The Reisberg et al. 2003 pivotal trial demonstrated a **6.1-point difference on the Severe Impairment Battery** (memantine: -4.0 vs placebo: -10.1, p<0.001) and improved activities of daily living by 1.3 points (p=0.02).

The 2019 Cochrane review, pooling 14 studies with approximately 3,700 participants, confirms high-certainty evidence for cognitive benefit: **3.11 points on the SIB** (95% CI: 2.42-3.92), 1.09 points on ADL measures (95% CI: 0.62-1.64), and 1.84 points on neuropsychiatric symptoms (95% CI: 1.05-2.78). Critically, the Cochrane reviewers found **no evidence of benefit in mild Alzheimer's disease** (MMSE >19)—memantine's efficacy is specific to moderate-to-severe stages.

For this patient with MMSE 13-17, memantine is well within its approved indication. The NNT ranges from 6-8 for single-domain improvement and as low as 3-4 in severe dementia (Winblad 1999). Its excellent tolerability profile—similar adverse event rates to placebo and no meaningful drug interactions—makes it safe to add to cholinesterase inhibitors.

## The DOMINO trial clarified combination therapy value

The 2012 DOMINO trial (Howard et al., NEJM) provided definitive evidence on combination therapy. In 295 patients with moderate-to-severe AD (SMMSE 5-13), continuing donepezil produced a **+1.9 SMMSE point benefit** (95% CI: 1.3-2.5, p<0.001) over discontinuation—exceeding the predefined minimum clinically important difference of 1.4 points. Adding memantine produced a smaller but significant **+1.2 point benefit** (95% CI: 0.6-1.8, p<0.001).

The critical and controversial finding: **no synergistic benefit** emerged from the donepezil-memantine combination. The drugs showed additive but not multiplicative effects. However, the Tariot et al. 2004 trial found that adding memantine to stable donepezil therapy produced significant cognitive benefits (SIB +3.4 points, p<0.001) and, notably, **fewer discontinuations** (7.4% vs 12.4%)—suggesting memantine may counteract some cholinergic side effects.

For this patient, reasonable options include donepezil monotherapy (best tolerability), memantine monotherapy (good tolerability, specifically indicated for her severity), or combination therapy (additive benefits, reasonable tolerability). Cost considerations favor generic donepezil and memantine, both now available inexpensively.

## Anti-amyloid therapies are not an option for moderate dementia

**Lecanemab (Leqembi) and donanemab (Kisunla) are FDA-approved only for MCI or mild Alzheimer's dementia—this patient with MMSE 13-17 would not be eligible.** This is perhaps the most important practical finding of this review.

The CLARITY AD trial for lecanemab (van Dyck et al., NEJM 2023) enrolled 1,795 patients with MMSE 22-30 and demonstrated a **0.45-point CDR-SB difference** (95% CI: -0.67 to -0.23), representing 27% slowing of decline. The TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2 trial for donanemab (Sims et al., JAMA 2023) showed a **0.67-point CDR-SB difference** (95% CI: -0.95 to -0.40), or 36% slowing in low/medium tau patients. Both effect sizes are small by Cohen's criteria (d = 0.21-0.23).

Notably, these effect sizes are **comparable to or smaller than** exercise interventions in established dementia. A meta-analysis of exercise in AD found Hedges' g = 0.46 on MMSE—more than double the standardized effect size of lecanemab. The anti-amyloid drugs also carry significant ARIA risks: lecanemab shows 12.6% ARIA-E (brain edema) and 17.3% ARIA-H (microhemorrhages); donanemab shows 24% ARIA-E and 31.3% ARIA-H. For APOE4 homozygotes, ARIA-E rates reach **32-41%**. Three deaths in the donanemab trial were directly linked to ARIA.

Given this patient's prior strokes and likely cerebrovascular disease, anti-amyloid therapies would be particularly concerning even if she met stage criteria—microhemorrhages are a contraindication, and the benefit-risk profile deteriorates substantially in the presence of cerebrovascular pathology.

## Vascular dementia has no approved treatments, making lifestyle paramount

**No drugs are FDA-approved specifically for vascular dementia in the United States.** This critical finding means all pharmacotherapy for this patient's vascular component is off-label. Donepezil has been studied most extensively in VaD, with trials showing **ADAS-cog improvements of 1.9-2.3 points**—approximately 30% smaller than its effects in pure Alzheimer's disease.

The Kavirajan and Schneider 2007 Lancet Neurology meta-analysis concluded that cholinesterase inhibitors and memantine produce "small benefits in cognition of uncertain clinical significance" in vascular dementia. The Román et al. 2010 donepezil trial (n=974) notably found **no significant benefit on global function** despite modest cognitive improvement—and raised a safety signal with 11 deaths in the donepezil group versus 0 in placebo.

For mixed Alzheimer's/vascular pathology, the picture is slightly more favorable. The Erkinjuntti et al. 2002 galantamine trial found **more robust responses in AD+CVD (mixed) than pure VaD**, suggesting the Alzheimer's component drives treatment response. This patient's mixed pathology may therefore respond better to cholinesterase inhibitors than pure vascular dementia would.

**Blood pressure control represents the strongest evidence base for vascular cognitive impairment.** The SPRINT-MIND trial demonstrated that intensive BP control (<120 mmHg systolic) reduced MCI risk by **19%** (HR 0.81, 95% CI: 0.69-0.95, p=0.007) and combined MCI/dementia by **15%** (HR 0.85, p=0.01). For a patient with prior strokes, this secondary prevention is disease-modifying in a way no current dementia medication can claim.

## Cognitive stimulation therapy matches drug efficacy at a fraction of the cost

The Spector et al. 2003 trial established CST as evidence-based treatment, showing significant MMSE improvement (p=0.044) and ADAS-cog improvement (p=0.014) in 201 patients with mild-to-moderate dementia. A 2022 meta-analysis of 10 RCTs found CST produces a **weighted mean difference of 1.98 MMSE points** (95% CI: 1.24-2.72, p<0.01)—comparable to or exceeding cholinesterase inhibitor effects.

The Cochrane review (Woods et al., 2023 update, 37 studies, n=2,766) confirms consistent cognitive benefits. Effect sizes range from **d = 0.35-0.58**, placing CST in the same efficacy range as pharmacotherapy. Crucially, CST also improves quality of life (WMD = 3.12 points) and communication abilities—outcomes not consistently achieved with medications.

Cost-effectiveness is exceptional. CST costs approximately **£20 per person** over 8 weeks, with a cost per QALY of £12,596-£19,573—well below NICE's £20,000 threshold. The incremental cost-effectiveness ratio is £75.32 per additional MMSE point, compared to substantially higher costs for pharmacotherapy. **CST combined with cholinesterase inhibitors is more cost-effective than medication alone**, according to health economic analyses.

Maintenance CST (weekly sessions after initial program) extends benefits to 6 months with an effect size of d = 1.0 compared to CST alone. For this patient, a 14-session CST program followed by weekly maintenance represents evidence-based, cost-effective treatment that can be implemented regardless of her mixed pathology.

## Exercise produces drug-equivalent cognitive effects

Multiple meta-analyses demonstrate that exercise interventions produce effect sizes comparable to pharmacotherapy. A 2025 meta-analysis of 45 RCTs found exercise improves MMSE by **SMD 0.30** (95% CI: 0.17-0.43) and MoCA by **SMD 0.39** (95% CI: 0.08-0.70). A head-to-head comparison meta-analysis (Pisani et al. 2021) found exercise in AD produces **Hedges' g = 0.46** on MMSE—directly comparable to donepezil's effects.

Multicomponent exercise programs (combining aerobic, resistance, and balance training) show the strongest effects, with one meta-analysis finding **SMD 1.17** (95% CI: 0.86-1.47) for combined programs. Optimal dosing appears to be ≥2 sessions weekly, 40-60 minutes per session, for programs lasting >23 weeks. Walking more than 2 hours weekly produced significant MMSE improvements in multiple studies.

For ADL maintenance—a critical concern for this patient's family—exercise shows **SMD 0.73** (95% CI: 0.23-1.23), substantially larger than any medication effect on functional outcomes. Physical capacity improvements (SMD 1.05) further support independence and reduce caregiver burden.

The mechanistic rationale is compelling: exercise increases BDNF (brain-derived neurotrophic factor), improves cerebrovascular function, reduces inflammation, and promotes neuroplasticity through pathways entirely different from cholinergic enhancement. This creates the theoretical basis for additive benefits when combined with pharmacotherapy.

## Combining drugs with lifestyle interventions produces additive benefits

The evidence for combining pharmacological and non-pharmacological interventions, while limited by lack of large factorial RCTs, consistently suggests **additive rather than synergistic effects**—both approaches contribute independent benefits that sum together.

Matsuda et al. (2007, 2010) found that donepezil plus CST prevented cognitive decline more effectively than donepezil alone, with significant group × time interactions favoring the combination. A 2024 retrospective study of 184 AD patients found donepezil plus rehabilitation training showed **higher total efficacy** (p<0.05) and higher BDNF levels than donepezil alone. Animal models (Shafaei et al. 2022) provide mechanistic support: neither donepezil nor environmental enrichment alone restored spatial memory in AD-model rats, but the **combination was effective**—with BDNF levels normalizing only in the combined treatment group.

For statin therapy specifically, a Swedish registry study found simvastatin users showed slower cognitive decline when combined with cholinesterase inhibitors. The lipophilic properties of simvastatin allow blood-brain barrier penetration, potentially providing neuroprotection through cholesterol homeostasis and protection against tau hyperphosphorylation. For this patient already on statins, **continuing statin therapy alongside dementia medications is supported by evidence** suggesting complementary mechanisms.

The FINGER trial (Ngandu et al., Lancet 2015) demonstrated that multicomponent lifestyle intervention (diet, exercise, cognitive training, vascular risk monitoring) produced **25% greater cognitive improvement** than health advice alone in 1,260 at-risk elderly over 2 years, with particularly strong effects on executive function (83% greater improvement) and processing speed (150% greater improvement). The effect size (Cohen's d ~0.13) is modest but meaningful, and the intervention addresses multiple pathological pathways simultaneously.

## Clinical recommendations for this specific patient

For a 75-year-old woman with MMSE 13-17, prior small strokes, high cholesterol on statins, and likely mixed Alzheimer's/vascular pathology, the evidence supports a comprehensive multimodal approach:

**Pharmacological foundation:**
- Continue or initiate **donepezil 10mg daily** (best-tolerated ChEI, evidence in both AD and VaD)
- Consider adding **memantine 20mg daily** (specifically indicated for moderate-severe AD, additive benefits, excellent tolerability)
- Continue **statin therapy** (cardiovascular/stroke prevention; possible cognitive co-benefit)
- Optimize **blood pressure control** to <130/80 mmHg or intensive target <120 systolic if tolerated (SPRINT-MIND evidence)

**Non-pharmacological interventions (equally important):**
- Enroll in **Cognitive Stimulation Therapy** program (14 sessions, effect size comparable to drugs, highly cost-effective)
- Implement **structured exercise program** (multicomponent: aerobic + resistance + balance; ≥2x/weekly, 40-60 minutes; effect size comparable to cholinesterase inhibitors)
- Consider **occupational therapy** assessment for ADL optimization (Graff protocol showed exceptional effect sizes, NNT = 1.3)
- Ensure **social engagement** through group-based activities (embedded in CST, associated with maintained function)

**What is NOT appropriate:**
- Anti-amyloid therapies (lecanemab, donanemab) are contraindicated—patient does not meet stage criteria (requires MMSE 20-30), and prior strokes/vascular pathology increase ARIA risk
- Dual antiplatelet therapy for cognitive benefit (no evidence; increased bleeding risk per SPS3 trial)

**Key message for the family:**
The evidence shows that lifestyle interventions are not merely "nice to have" but produce **effect sizes equivalent to FDA-approved medications**. Exercise (Hedges' g = 0.46) and CST (d = 0.35-0.58) match or exceed cholinesterase inhibitors (d = 0.30-0.40). Blood pressure control is the only intervention with evidence for actually modifying vascular cognitive impairment progression. The optimal approach combines pharmacotherapy with structured lifestyle interventions—not as alternatives, but as **complementary strategies working through different mechanisms** that together provide the best chance of maintaining function and quality of life.

---

## Conclusion: The evidence demands a multimodal approach

The comparative evidence is clear: **lifestyle interventions deserve equal standing with pharmacotherapy** in dementia treatment. For moderate-stage mixed Alzheimer's/vascular dementia, the effect sizes of exercise (SMD 0.30-0.46), cognitive stimulation (d 0.35-0.58), and occupational therapy (d 2.3-2.5) are comparable to or exceed those of donepezil (d 0.31-0.40) and memantine (d 0.21-0.36). The new anti-amyloid drugs, despite their high cost and media attention, are not indicated for this patient's disease stage and show effect sizes (d 0.21-0.23) no larger than exercise.

For vascular and mixed pathology, the absence of FDA-approved treatments makes aggressive vascular risk factor management—particularly blood pressure control—the most evidence-based intervention available. The SPRINT-MIND trial's demonstration of **19% MCI risk reduction** with intensive BP control represents genuine disease modification that no current dementia drug can claim.

The family should understand that combining donepezil and/or memantine with structured exercise, CST, and vascular risk optimization represents the current evidence-based standard of care—not because lifestyle interventions are "alternative medicine," but because **the clinical trial evidence puts them on equal footing with pharmaceuticals**. The expected trajectory remains one of gradual decline, but a comprehensive multimodal approach offers the best chance of preserving function, maintaining quality of life, and supporting both patient and caregivers through the journey ahead.
